# Discussion

In this benchmark on a public four-centre dataset, no pre-specified T2WI model detected muscle invasion better than tumour size once evaluation leakage was removed. On held-out centres, the radiomics and fusion models matched size, and the fine-tuned CNN performed worse. By contrast, single evaluation flaws applied to the same models produced AUCs of up to {{CNN_L1_A}}, at the top of the range reported for this task.

Our leakage-free AUCs are close to the 0.82–0.87 reported for FedBCa with centralised or federated training [@fedbca; @jiim_fed] and below most single-centre reports and the pooled estimate of 0.88 [@rad_meta]. Tumour size explains much of this performance: the median MIBC volume was {{VOL_RATIO}} times the median NMIBC volume, and a two-feature size model reached {{A_S2_SIZE}} on held-out centres. Size-related features also dominated feature selection: the {{TOPFREQ_FEAT_PROSE}}, selected in {{TOPFREQ_FRAC}} of folds, correlated with log tumour volume at ρ = {{TOPFREQ_RHO}}, and {{SHARE_SEL_SIZECORR}} of all selections were features with |ρ| ≥ 0.5. A size comparator therefore belongs in every such study.

Slice-level splitting was the most damaging flaw. Adjacent T2WI slices of one tumour are nearly identical, so a network tested on slices whose neighbours it was trained on largely recognises tumours it has already seen [@yagis; @tampu]. This flaw alone produced an AUC of {{CNN_L1_A}}, whereas the same network scored {{CNN_SLICE_A}} on slices of unseen patients. Splits that ignored patient grouping mattered less here because few patients had more than one tumour; in cohorts with more multifocal disease this flaw would leak more.

The other flaws were smaller but not benign. Selecting features on all tumours added {{RAD_L3_DS}} to the radiomics AUC, enough to make a model that is no better than size appear superior; reporting the best of {{N_L5_CONFIGS}} configurations added only {{RAD_L5_DS}}, because their AUCs spanned a narrow range ({{L5_RANGE}}). Choosing the CNN epoch by test AUC changed little in cross-validation but added {{CNN_LOCOL5_DS}} on held-out centres, where validation data from the training centres poorly reflected the new centre. External validation therefore offers no protection when the test centre informs any modelling choice. Harmonisation needs the same care: ComBat fitted with the outcome as covariate writes a label term into every feature, so the outcome must never enter a harmonisation step that includes test data.

Centre information was pervasive. Radiomics features identified the acquiring centre with a macro AUC of {{ID_RAD}}, first-order intensity features alone with {{ID_FO}} and frozen deep features with {{ID_BCLIP}}–{{ID_DINO}}. Because MIBC prevalence differed between centres ({{PREV_RANGE}}), the centre alone yielded an AUC of {{A_S1_CENTRE}} in pooled cross-validation, so pooled internal validation can reward models that recognise the centre [@zech; @degrave]. On held-out centres, the CNN's predicted risk did not follow centre prevalence, which lowered its pooled AUC ({{A_S2_CNN}}) below the random-effects summary of its per-centre AUCs ({{RE_A_CNN}}), and calibration, not discrimination, gave radiomics its higher net benefit than size. Calibration therefore needs to be assessed per centre before any clinical use [@vancalster].

The interpretability analyses point the same way. The radiomics model relied on size-related shape and texture features whose selection varied between folds. The CNN's attention was only modestly concentrated on the tumour. Blurring everything outside the tumour reduced its AUC more than blurring the tumour itself, whereas blurring the 3-mm ring, which includes the adjacent bladder wall assessed by VI-RADS, had little effect. The network therefore drew substantially on image content beyond the tumour and its immediate wall.

These results suggest five safeguards for MRI studies of muscle invasion, consistent with TRIPOD+AI, CLEAR and METRICS [@tripod_ai; @clear; @metrics_tool]: split data and aggregate predictions at the patient level; keep every data-dependent step, including feature selection, harmonisation, epoch and threshold choice, inside the training data; name the primary model before testing; evaluate on held-out centres and report per-centre AUCs with random-effects summaries and prediction intervals; and report a tumour-size comparator. Releasing code, splits and predictions lets readers verify each of these.

This study has limitations. First, it relies on one public dataset with four centres, so between-centre estimates are imprecise and prediction intervals are wide. Second, only T2WI is released; VI-RADS also uses diffusion-weighted and contrast-enhanced imaging, and multiparametric models may perform better. Third, no reader assessments, clinical variables or follow-up are available, and pathological staging by TURBT may itself under-stage some tumours [@understaging]. Fourth, all models require a manual segmentation, and our robustness analysis covered only small contour changes. Fifth, the CNN was evaluated on one cross-validation repeat, and GPU training is not bit-for-bit reproducible. Sixth, each audit variant reproduces a generic form of a flaw; it shows how much that flaw can inflate performance, not what any particular study did. Finally, with {{N_MIBC}} events the cohort is smaller than recommended for developing a prediction model [@riley2020], so all models should be regarded as exploratory.

In conclusion, on a public four-centre dataset, T2WI radiomics and deep learning did not detect muscle invasion better than tumour size once evaluation leakage was removed, whereas single evaluation flaws reproduced the high AUCs reported in the literature. New MRI models for muscle invasion should be compared with tumour size on held-out centres.
